1 Introduction
Reusing software components (code dependencies, containers, pre-trained AI models, etc.) speeds up and modularizes the engineering of software products.  This reuse process creates a complex software supply chain and introduces potential attack vectors, such as unauthorized (and often malicious) code injections. To mitigate this class of risk, the integrity of artifacts and the authenticity of actors must be established throughout the supply chain; this is called provenance.  Software signing provides the strongest formally proven guarantee of provenance in practice.  Software signing cryptographically links a software artifact to its origin by binding the artifact to an issuer's identity and enabling downstream consumers to verify integrity, authenticity, and traceability. In practice, software signing spans two coupled workflows: upstream signature creation by producers and downstream signature verification by consumers. If either workflow is difficult to execute correctly, the overall guarantee degrades because producers may mis-sign or avoid signing, and consumers may fail to verify or stop verifying.Historically, many signing tools have used key-managed designs in which users generate and protect a long-lived public-private key pair, as in OpenPGP-style workflows.
Usable security research shows that when security tools impose complex key management and error-prone steps, users adopt workarounds, and the effective security guarantee weakens. Prior work shows that signing workflows can fail in practice due to integration friction, confusing verification semantics, and organizational constraints, even when cryptographic primitives are sound. To address these barriers, tool designers have shifted toward identity-based approaches that rely on identity providers and short-lived credentials, aiming to reduce key-handling burden while preserving verifiability. However, there is limited empirical evidence on how the usability of identity-based signing tools evolves after introduction, including whether early usability problems are resolved, displaced, or persist as tools mature. This gap matters for organizations deciding whether to adopt these tools and for tool makers deciding which usability problems to prioritize as deployments scale. This knowledge can help organizations (including potential adopters) reason about where these tools are in their adoption lifecycle, what usability problems are most likely to surface at that stage, and how ready they are to adopt such tools, shaping adoption decisions for identity-based signing. It can also help tool makers prioritize improvements that reduce friction as deployments scale.
In this study, we empirically examine the usability lifecycle of identity-based signing tools by analyzing how usability concerns evolve in public GitHub issue discussions.We treat issues and their follow-on comments as a time-stamped record of reported challenges to the effective use of each tool, and we analyze these reports comparatively across tools. We analyze 3{,}901 issues over a 48-month window ending on November~5,~2025, and we link each issue with its comments to preserve context while excluding bot-generated issues.We study five identity-based signing ecosystems:Sigstore, OpenPubKey, HashiCorp Vault, Keyfactor,and Notary~v2. We code each issue for the type of usability concern it reflects and for the tool component(s) implicated by the report and ensuing discussion. We then analyze how these coded concerns distribute across tools and how they shift over time using regression analyses. 

2 Background and Related Work
2.1 Software signing
Software signing is a formally guaranteed method of establishing provenance by ensuring authorship and artifact integrity. It builds on public key cryptography, where two complementary functions exist: a signer Figure 1: Traditional key-managed signing centers on a long-lived key pair that the signer creates and protects, and that verifiers must obtain to validate signatures. The workflow highlights two recurring usability burdens: public-key distribution (often via a key server) and establishing confidence that the retrieved public key corresponds to the intended issuer identity holds a private key and distributes a corresponding public key, and a verifier verifies the identity of the signer using the distributed public key. When verification succeeds, the verifier gains evidence that: the artifact has not been modified since signing, and the signer controlled the corresponding private key at signing time. In practice, these guarantees depend not only on cryptographic primitives but also on the surrounding workflow that governs: how signing keys or credentials are created, how they are protected, how they are rotated and revoked, how verifiers discover the correct verification material, and how identities are bound to verification material.
Software signing tools operationalize these tasks by providing user interfaces, automation, and infrastructure that bridge between the abstract cryptographic model and developer workflows. As a result, the evolution of signing tools largely reflects changes in how tool ecosystems allocate responsibility for key management and identity binding. We next describe two tool families, key-managed (legacy) and identity-based signing tools, and the workflows they impose on signers and verifiers.
2.1.1 Key-managed (legacy) software signing tools
Key-managed (legacy) software signing tools, which Schorlemmer et al. describe as traditional signing tools-such as OpenPGP-style workflows-rely on long-lived public-private key pairs that are created and maintained by the signer. A typical workflow has three phases. First, the signer generates a key pair and protects the private key. Second, the signer distributes the public key (or certificate) so that verifiers can discover it. Third, verifiers retrieve the artifact, retrieve the signer's public information, and validate the signature before trusting the artifact. This key-managed design makes usability hinge on key lifecycle tasks that are external to the signing act itself. Examples include key generation choices, backups, rotation, revocation,
Usable security research shows that these steps are difficult to execute correctly, and difficulty can reduce adoption or lead to incorrect verification decisions.

2.1.2 Identity-based software signing tools
Identity-based signing tools aim to reduce direct user key management by shifting responsibility for identity binding and credential issuance to an ecosystem of components. Instead of requiring developers to create and distribute long- lived keys, identity-based ecosystems typically rely on: an orchestrator that performs signing and verification, an identity provider that authenticates the signer, a credential-issuing service such as a Certificate Authority (CA) that binds identity to a signing credential, and an optional logging infrastructure that supports transparency and audit. Examples of tools here include; Sigstore, A key strength of identity-based tool design is this decoupling of responsibilities into components. The same signing CLI can often be configured to work with different credential issuers, and organizations can reuse existing PKI services or logging infrastructure rather than adopting a single monolithic system. For example, a team might use a Sigstore-style CLI experience for signing while relying on an enterprise CA implementation for credential issuance, or they may adopt a Keyfactor-style issuer while integrating verification through other downstream tooling. This modularity increases flexibility, but it also expands the configuration surface and introduces cross-component usability problems at integration boundaries. orchestrator. The orchestrator is the primary interface that developers use to sign and verify artifacts and to integrate signing into CI/CD pipelines. In the workflow shown in Figure 2, the CLI creates or selects the artifact to be signed (A), obtains an identity token from an IdP (B-C), initiatesthe process to generate an ephemeral key pair (D-E), creates the artifact signature (G), and associates the resulting signature with the credential material needed for verification. Examples of orchestrators in popular identity-based software signing ecosystems include: Sigstore cosign, Notary v2 notation, and OpenPubKey tooling, while some ecosystems may provide alternative entry points or embed signing into platform tooling rather than a standalone CLI. Identity provider. The identity provider authenticates a human or workload and issues an identity token that the CLI canpresent to other services. In practice, these providers are often standards-based systems such as OpenID Connect providers, and are not implemented by the signing tools themselves. Examples include enterprise IdPs (e.g., Okta) and developer ecosystem identities (e.g., Google or GitHub-based authentication), depending on tool configuration and deployment model. Certificate authority or credential issuer. A credential issuing service binds the authenticated identity to signing material, commonly by issuing a short-lived certificate for an ephemeral public key (E-F). This step enables verification to rely on an identity binding that is time-scoped and auditable, rather than on a long-lived key that must be distributed and trusted indefinitely. Examples include Sigstore deployments that use a dedicated issuing service-Fulcio-for identity certificates and enterprise deployments that use organizational PKI components such as EJBCA as the issuer. Certificate and signature logs. Many identity-based ecosystems include append-only logs that record issuance events and signing events to support transparency, monitoring, and audit e.g., Sigstore's Rekor. The workflow records certificate-related information (G-I) and signature-related information (H-J) so that verifiers can consult these records as part of verification. These logs are not always mandatory for every deployment, but they often shape verification semantics, debugging practices, and operational requirements.

2.2 Software Signing Usability
2.2.1 Usability Concepts
Usability concerns the extent to which a tool enables users to achieve its intended purpose, that is, to meet product goals. In this paper, we consider two aspects of usability: summative and formative. Summative usability assesses whether users can achieve specified goals effectively, efficiently, and satisfactorily in a given context, using metrics such as effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction. Formative usability diagnoses difficulties users encounter and identifies areas for improvement to support product evolution. Tool choice can affect a developer's workflow efficiency, productivity, and the quality of the resulting work. These considerations shape decisions about selecting tools for software engineering and security tasks. These factors are positively related to dimensions of usability of such a tool and thus usability invariably influences technology and tool adoption and acceptance. This role of usability has been noted in several software engineering and security- related contexts and also plays a role for software signing. These adoption dynamics motivate asking how usability changes as tools evolve and mature.

2.2.2 Usability: Technology Maturity Theory
Technology maturity refers to the extent of a technology's development, including its current state, the amount of development remaining, its predicted supportability, and its interoperability with existing systems. A common expectation in tooling is that usability improves as technologies mature because iterative development incorporates user feedback and stabilizes interfaces and workflows. Prior work also suggests that usability often improves as adoption expands beyond innovators to more pragmatic users, interaction patterns stabilize, and user-centered design practices become more systematic in organizations and by general usage. This expectation is visible in developer centered security as well, where early tools can be difficult to use correctly and later iterations can reduce misuse when design and documentation better align with developer practice. However, maturity does not guarantee uniform improvement across workflows, because new deployment contexts and scaling pressures can surface new usability problems even as earlier ones are resolved. This maturity perspective motivates treating usability as an evolving property rather than a one-time evaluation result. Accordingly, in this work, we examine whether the incidence and composition of developer-reported usability problems changes over time and across identity-based signing tools (and their composite components), rather than assuming that newer architectures are uniformly easier to use. In this sense, our work aligns with formative evaluation goals, where the objective is to surface actionable improvement targets that can guide continued refinement of security tooling in practice. We next summarize evidence from signing tools and usable-security studies that illustrates both the promise of improved usability and the persistence of workflow breakdowns in practice.

2.2.3 Usability of Signing Tools
Prior work on software signing similarly emphasizes that signing effectiveness and adoption outcomes depend on the usability of signing tools and the surrounding adoption and deployment practices, not only on the correctness of cryptographic primitives. The seminal work by Whitten and Tygar and subsequent usability studies of cryptographic tools underscore that key-managed tools such as PGP can be difficult to use correctly, including for key verification, transparency, and signing, and that the public-key model itself can impose complexity. However, these works primarily study signing in the context of secure communication (email and messaging), leaving software signing comparatively underexplored.
To study signing usability in the context of software distribution, Kalu et al. present two related studies on software signing usability. Their study of Sigstore as an exemplar identity-based signing tool shows that next-generation workflows can reduce key-management burdens while still facing usability problems related to integration, transparency tradeoffs, and organizational deployment constraints. Their findings also suggest that different components within an identity-based tool can exhibit different readiness levels, reflected in component-specific usability challenges, which can shape adoption risk. This complements their earlier work, which highlighted the role of tooling usability in organizational software signing implementations. Relatedly, Schorlemmer et al. report limited effective use of software signing in open-source software ecosystems.  Our work builds on and differs from these lines of work in three ways. First, instead of focusing on a single tool via interviews alone, we compare multiple identity-based signing tools using a uniform observational lens. Second, we examine usability issues beyond those surfaced in organizational implementations, which can omit problems that arise in public adoption and maintenance contexts. Third, building on Kalu et al.'s evidence that tools and components can exhibit different maturity levels as reflected in usability challenges, we study the usability lifecycle through longitudinal issue discussions. This approach complements interview-based accounts by capturing usability problems as they arise during integration, maintenance, and evolution.  
2.3 Motivating Observations From Tool Communities 
Across identity-based software signing ecosystems, community meetings of popular identity-based tools such as Notary, Sigstore, and OpenPubKey repeatedly surface usability con- cerns that can impede effective tool use in practice, including configuration and CLI friction, developer experience challenges when using tools as APIs and integrating them into CI/CD, gaps in diagnostics and documentation that make failures hard to understand and recover from, and contributor-facing friction around project status, onboarding, and stability expectations. These concerns connect directly to the background in §2.1 and §2.2: signing guarantees de- pend on end-to-end workflows, and usability shapes whether developers can successfully adopt, operate, and sustain these workflows. While these concerns are visible in meeting notes and community exchanges, the scale and distribution of these usability problems across tools and components, how they evolve over time, and what they imply about the maturity of these tools remain unclear. This gap motivates our study, which characterizes the types of developer-reported usability problems and affected components across identity-based signing tools and examines how these concerns shift as tools mature. 
3 Research Questions and Hypotheses 
Prior work shows that usability problems can weaken the effective security of software signing in practice, even when cryptographic primitives are sound.In response, tool designers have shifted from key-managed designs to- ward identity-based software signing tools that aim to reduce key-management burden and improve usability and reduce adoptability burdens. However, we lack cross-tool evidence about the usability problems developers report for these tools and whether these problems diminish or change form as the tools mature. We address this gap by analyzing developer-reported us- ability problems in public issue discussions across multiple identity-based software signing tools over time. We pose two research questions. • RQ1: What usability problems do developers report when adopting and using identity-based software signing tools? This question identifies the recurring categories of usability problems reported for each tool. • RQ2: Which identity-based software signing tool components mostly report usability problems? This question identifies the tool components most often implicated in developer reports. • RQ3: How do developer-reported usability problems change over time as identity-based software signing tools mature? This question evaluates whether reported usability problems decline over time and whether the trajectory differs across tools. To operationalize RQ3, we state two working hypotheses based on the roles of usability in technology maturity (§2.2.2). These hypotheses connect the qualitative notion of tool matu- rity to observable trends in reported usability issue frequency. • H1: As identity-based software signing tools (and their components) mature, the frequency of reported usability issues decreases over time. • H2: The rate of decrease in reported usability issues would differ across tools, with more mature tools exhibiting stronger downward trends.

4.1.2 Data Source: GitHub Issues as Usability Evidence 
We use GitHub issues and discussions reports and their associated comment threads to study developer-reported usability problems in identity-based software signing tools. Issue reports are a natural data source for this study because they capture problems encountered during real adoption, integration, and maintenance work, and they preserve the context of troubleshooting and resolution through discussion. This source is also aligned with our goal of characterizing usability problems that arise in practice, rather than usability judgments elicited through controlled tasks. As is standard in mining software repositories research, we treat issue discussions as situated evidence of developer experience rather than as a complete record of all problems encountered. Prior usability work has also used GitHub issues as a source of usability evidence. Other work has used similar clarification platforms, such as Stack Overflow, to study developer difficulties and questions. Relatedly, prior work has shown that issue tracking platforms can surface usability issues in broader open-source development settings.
4.1.3 Issue Collection
We collect GitHub issues from the selected repositories over a 48-month window ending on November 5, 2025. We choose this window to enable longitudinal analysis while keeping the dataset within a fixed time horizon in which tool workflows and developer platforms are broadly comparable. Because most of these tools are relatively young, we focus on a shorter window rather than sampling across the full repository history. For each issue, we retain issue metadata and link the issue to its comment thread to preserve troubleshooting and resolution context. We exclude bot-generated issues to focus our analysis on developer-reported usability problems. In total, our corpus contains approximately 3.9k issues spanning the selected component repositories across the five tools, with per-repository counts summarized in Table 1.
4.2 Data Processing and Coding
In this section, we describe how we annotated issues and scaled coding with Generative AI and LLMs [66]. We first establish an annotation scheme through human pair annotation. We then apply LLM assistance to transfer this scheme across the full corpus.
4.2.1 Establishing an Issue Annotation Scheme: Human Pair Annotation We began issue processing by progressively developing a characterization framework for issue content, usability relevance, and usability themes. Two analysts performed this phase of the study, Analysts A and B1. To develop the framework, we selected a stratified sample of 180 issues, representing approximately 5% of the 3.9k-issue corpus. We allocated samples proportionally across repositories and further stratified by time (early, middle, late thirds of the window) and by discussion intensity (short versus long comment threads). We also diversified across issue labels (e.g., bug, enhancement, question, documentation) to avoid over-sampling a single issue type, using labels as a guide rather than as ground truth. Calibrating Issue Understanding Between Analysts. To establish a shared understanding of identity-based signing concepts and issue context, both analysts independently memoed a subset of 50 issues from the 180-issue sample. For each issue, we used the title, labels, body, and comments to memo the affected component functionality and a plain-language description of what the issue reported. We computed percentage agreement to assess semantic alignment between analysts. Across the 50 issues, analysts reached 90% agreement on affected component functionality and 88% agreement on issue understanding. These results indicated that the memo fields and heuristics provided sufficient context for subsequent coding. Calibrating Usability Versus Non-Usability Issues. Next, we distinguished usability issues from non-usability issues. We define usability using the summative and formative lens described in §2.2.1, and we treat an issue as usability-relevant when the tool is not blocked primarily by a functional defect but the user cannot achieve goals efficiently, effectively, or satisfactorily due to workflow design, interface behavior, feedback, or documentation. When issue reports were ambiguous, we relied on follow-on discussion and the apparent resolution to infer the primary barrier rather than relying only on the initial report. Both analysts independently labeled a subset of 100 issues as usability or non-usability based on these criteria2. We report analyst agreement for this step using percentage agreement, and we resolve disagreements through discussion and codebook refinement. Generating Lower-Level Usability Themes.
